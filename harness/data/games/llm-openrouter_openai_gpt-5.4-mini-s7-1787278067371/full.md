1. I opened with economy and early rig setup, then shifted into repeated central pressure, mostly HQ runs, while building toward a workable decoder breaker. I did manage to steal Offworld Office and keep the Corp under some pressure, but I spent too much time trading clicks for incremental value instead of converting that into a decisive scoring window.

2. My plan was to build cheap economy, install a basic rig, and then use central runs to find or steal agendas before the Corp stabilized. As the game developed, I got drawn into a slower tempo pattern: lots of open HQ runs, repeated damage checks, and several turns spent rebuilding credits instead of applying real server pressure. I also overvalued some low-impact economy clicks late, which left me unable to break through Diviner safely.

3. The first big turning point was the early HQ access into Urtica Cipher, which gave me little payoff but showed I could pressure open HQ. The second was installing Unity and getting to the point where I could at least contest R&D. The biggest turning point was the Corp rezzing Diviner on HQ: after that, my repeated HQ attacks became much riskier, and once I started losing key event cards to net damage, my hand collapsed. The final turning point was when I chose to boost Unity on Diviner while holding an odd-cost card in grip; that handed the Corp the flatline.

4. I would have been more selective with early clicks and tried to find a stronger economic breakpoint sooner, so I could threaten a real run instead of just poking open centrals. I also would have been more cautious about repeated HQ runs into unknown or rezzable ICE once I knew the Corp had damage tools. Most importantly, I’d have avoided getting into a position where an odd-cost card in grip could be randomly trashed during a damage subroutine with only one card left.

5. Mostly the interface was clear, especially for runs and the step-by-step access windows. The biggest challenge was that my own hand state and the random damage risk weren’t always easy to mentally track across many small interactions, so it was easy to miss the danger of having an odd-cost card in grip. Also, the repeated run subwindows made it easy to overcommit to continuing a line even when it had become strategically bad.
